# Art direction

Modern 16-bit inspired pixel art. Bigger and more expressive than NES-era sprites, with
crisp outlines, a warm dusk palette and enough detail in the street that a block feels
lived in.

All of it is original and generated by the scripts in `tools/`.

---

## Rules

**Everything gets an outline.** Characters are drawn twice per limb: once expanded in a
near-black `(24, 16, 28)`, once filled. Props run through `outline_alpha()`, which walks the
alpha channel and darkens any transparent pixel touching an opaque one. That single pass is
most of what makes a generated prop set look like one set.

**Three tones per surface.** A base colour, a shade at about 75%, and a highlight at about
120% on the top edge. Enough to read as form at 48 px, not so much that it turns muddy.

**Never pure black or pure white.** The darkest value in the game is `(24, 16, 28)` and the
lightest is around `(250, 248, 240)`. Both carry a hue, which keeps the palette warm.

**Silhouette first.** Characters are separated by shape before colour: the heavy is wider
with a mask, the rusher is thinner with a cap, the boss is broad with a flat-top.

**Nearest-neighbour, always.** `default_texture_filter=0` project-wide, plus pixel snapping
on transforms and vertices.

## Characters

Roughly 48 px tall in a 64 px frame, so about 18% of the 270 px screen height. Big enough
for readable animation, small enough to fit five fighters on screen.

Built from a rig: head, torso, front and back arm, front and back leg, each limb three
joints. Drawing order changes per pose, so a punch can bring the back arm in front of the
torso.

Faces are minimal on purpose: one or two pixels for eyes, one line for a mouth. At this
size, expression comes from body language.

**Outfit styles** — jacket, hoodie, track, sweater, overalls, vest, suit, gi, shirt — each
draw differently on the torso: an open jacket shows an undershirt strip, a track top gets
racing stripes, a sweater gets horizontal bands, a suit gets lapels and a tie.

**Hair styles** — short, spiky, side, slick, long, bun, mohawk, afro, flattop, bald, plus
caps, beanies, bandanas and helmets.

Every character is drawn from the same renderer: a skeleton of joint positions, capsule
limbs, a torso polygon and a head circle. Three rules that turned out to matter more than
any individual design choice:

- **Each part outlines with its own fill.** Outlining everything first and then filling
  everything lets a later part erase an earlier part's rim, which is what made arms
  disappear into torsos.
- **Eyes are two pixels wide and sit clear of the fringe.** One dark pixel is invisible at
  this size, and an eye flush against the hairline merges into it.
- **The key light is up and to the left**, on every part, without exception.

**A limb needs a joint.** A uniform-width capsule in one flat tone reads as a slab, not a
limb, and the bigger the character the worse it gets. Forearms are slimmer than upper
arms, and every limb is broken at the elbow even when there is no sleeve to justify it.
Arms hang from the edge of the shoulder, so a wider torso carries them outward.

**Silhouette carries the role.** Torso width, limb thickness, head size, scale, stance and
hunch come from the enemy's archetype: a rusher is small and narrow, a grappler broad and
planted, a heavy widest with a small head against a big body, which is what sells mass.
Read the outline with the colour removed and the roles should still be obvious.

The Commuters share one navy palette on purpose. Chapter one's gangs are five different
looks; the sixth is one look repeated, which is the joke and also how you read them at a
glance in a crowd.

NPCs are built the same way: Dez in a yellow shirt with an afro, Auntie Mae with a bun and
an apron, Odell bald in a white gi with a black belt, Marisol with glasses and long hair,
Bex in a grey vest with wrapped hands, Nadia in an apron behind a shutter.

## Environment

**Buildings** are drawn as facades with a roof lip, textured walls (brick courses, panel
seams or stucco noise), windows lit at random, and a ground-floor storefront with an angled
glass reflection, a door and a sign. Signage uses abstract blocks rather than letters, which
keeps it legible at any size and language-neutral.

**Streets** are built in bands: road at the back, a curb line, then the sidewalk the player
walks on. Cars and fences sit on the road, street lights stand on the sidewalk's back edge,
props sit at their own lane depth.

**Parallax** runs three or four layers deep: a sky gradient at 0.02, a far skyline at 0.25,
a near skyline at 0.5, and for Ferry Row a river band at 0.12. The far layers are tinted
toward the sky so distance reads as haze.

**Props** are drawn to be recognisable in one glance at 20 px: a hydrant is a red column
with a cap and two side ports, a dumpster is a green box with a lighter lid.

**Areas with no street behind them need their own placement rules.** Street scenery sits at
building height, well above the walkable lane, because a building is there to hold it. On
the Rooftop Route there is no building behind the roof, so anything placed at that height
floats in open sky. Roof scenery stands on the parapet line instead. Interiors have the
same problem in reverse: the Metro Platform's tunnel wall is a parallax layer drawn at 1:1,
because at the 2x scale a street sky uses, one tunnel mouth fills the whole screen.

**Alternating floor tiles are for outdoors.** The `alt` tile option reads as paving
variation on a sidewalk and as a chessboard indoors. Both interior floors use a single
tile. The station floor's own two tones were pulled close together for the same reason:
under area lighting a 40% value split stopped reading as a floor and started reading as a
chessboard. Tiling reads from the grout line now.

## Light

Areas are lit by data, not by painting. An area layout may carry a `lighting` block with
an ambient tint and a set of light pools; an area without one is drawn flat, exactly as
everything was before lighting existed.

**Ambient is the time of day.** It is a `CanvasModulate`, so it multiplies everything.
The Metro Platform is underground, so its ambient is cool and low and every warm pixel in
the frame is something somebody installed.

**What glows is decided by the generator, not by the renderer.** `gen_emission.py` derives
a mask of the light-emitting pixels of each asset, and the engine draws that mask again on
top at a gain above 1.0. With HDR 2D on and the bloom threshold at exactly 1.0, ordinary
art can never bloom, so a white sneaker stays a white sneaker and only a lamp glows.

**Gains are per asset and deliberately unequal.** A lamp head is a light source and runs
hot. A ticket screen or a shop sign is a lit surface, not a lamp, and runs about half as
hard; at lamp gain they clamped into featureless white blobs.

**Emission is compensated for ambient.** A dark area multiplies emission down along with
everything else, so a lamp would stop clearing the bloom threshold exactly when the scene
got dark enough to need it. The gain is divided by the ambient luminance.

## Effects

| Effect | Look |
|---|---|
| Light spark | Small white core with six short yellow rays, 4 frames |
| Heavy spark | Larger, orange, eight rays |
| Weapon spark | Cool blue-white, seven rays |
| Special | Big, violet, ten rays |
| Dust | Three grey puffs drifting up and fading |
| Shadow | Soft ellipse under every character, shrinking with jump height |
| Stars | Yellow stars bursting outward on a knockout |
| Numbers | Floating damage in gold, larger and orange on a crit |

Every effect is a four-frame strip stepped by a tween and freed on completion, so nothing
accumulates.

## UI

The interface is built in code against `UITheme`, which owns the palette and widget
styling. Panels are flat with a two-pixel border and a lighter top edge. Bars are three
bands: highlight, base, shade.

The HUD is deliberately quiet: level and vitals top-left, money top-right, current quest
under it in dim text. The boss bar only appears during a boss fight, notifications rise
above centre-bottom and fade, and the interact prompt only shows when something is in reach.

Touch buttons are translucent circles with a pictograph — a fist, a boot, an arrow, a hand,
a star — so they read without labels and never fully hide the street.

---

## Deliberate limits

- **No rotation on characters.** Everything is axis-aligned pixel art; rotation would
  produce shimmer.
- **No lighting or normal maps.** Shading is painted in.
- **No shaders** beyond the default canvas material, which keeps mobile browsers happy.
- **One atlas for ground tiles**, one sheet per character.
- **Signage is abstract**, avoiding a font dependency and translation work.
